Conversational Models

Open the door. Start where you are. Grow from there.

You do not need to be an expert—or even comfortable talking about faith—to begin. Learn how to start meaningful conversations, engage at your current stage of preparedness, and follow up without pressure.

Start a conversation ↓ Find your stage
The point

You do not have to win the moment.

Sometimes the best response is a question. Sometimes it is, “That’s a good question—let me get back to you.” The goal is not instant mastery. The goal is faithful engagement over time.

Starting spiritual conversations

Meaningful conversations often begin with one good question.

You do not need to force a transition or deliver a presentation. A thoughtful question can reveal what someone believes, invite reflection, and show you where a future conversation might naturally go.

Meaning & Purpose
“What do you think gives life meaning?”

Possible directions: purpose, human value, happiness, vocation, God.

Death & Eternity
“What do you think happens when we die?”

Possible directions: afterlife, soul, naturalism, judgment, hope, Jesus.

Morality
“Where do you think our sense of right and wrong comes from?”

Possible directions: objective morality, culture, human nature, God.

Truth
“Do you think truth is something we discover or create?”

Possible directions: relativism, evidence, knowledge, competing truth claims.

God
“What has shaped the way you think about God?”

Possible directions: upbringing, suffering, church experiences, atheism, belief.

Christianity
“What do you think Christianity gets right—and what do you think it gets wrong?”

Possible directions: Jesus, church, morality, hypocrisy, Bible, salvation.

Prepare without scripting.

If you plan to ask a question like “What do you think happens when we die?”, you can think ahead about several likely directions the answer may take. You are not memorizing a script—you are simply becoming more ready to listen well and recognize opportunities.

The goal is not to force a conversation. The goal is to open a door—and be willing to walk through it when someone does.
Different stages of preparedness

You can begin before you feel prepared.

Some believers feel completely unprepared and uncomfortable. Others have spent years thinking about these topics. The goal is not to jump immediately to expertise. Start where you are and grow over time.

Stage One

No knowledge. No comfort.

Listen. Notice the claim or question. You do not need to respond on the spot.

“That’s a good question. I’m not sure how I’d answer that right now. Let me think about it and get back to you.”

Later, when the pressure is off, explore the issue and follow up.

Stage Two

Growing comfort. Limited knowledge.

Ask thoughtful questions. Clarify the claim, test the reasoning, and let the person who made the claim carry the burden of explaining it.

“What do you mean by that?”   “How did you come to that conclusion?”

Keep asking until your comfort runs out or you reach a natural stopping point—then research and follow up later.

Stage Three

Knowledge and confidence together.

Combine strategic questions with clear responses, corrections, worldview analysis, and deeper discussion.

Questions still matter—but now you can also explain why the claim succeeds or fails.

You may reach this stage in some topics long before others. That is normal.

The power of questions

Questions do more than gather information.

They can clarify a claim, expose hidden assumptions, reveal contradictions, and keep the burden of proof on the person making the assertion.

Clarify“What do you mean by that?”
Ask for reasons“How did you come to that conclusion?”
Test consistency“Would that apply in every case?”
Expose implications“If that were true, what would follow?”
Modeled interactions

See what each stage looks like.

These are not scripts. They are examples designed to show how the same kinds of conversations can be handled differently depending on your knowledge and comfort.

Stage One

Listen now. Work through it later.

The Bible has been changed

Coworker: You can’t trust the Bible. It’s been changed so many times.

You: That’s interesting.

Later: You look into manuscript transmission and Bible translations, then follow up: “I was thinking about what you said the other day. I found a short explanation that I thought was interesting.”
Science disproves God

Friend: Science has basically disproved God anyway.

You: That’s an interesting thought.

Later: You spend time learning about the issue, then say: “Remember when you mentioned science disproving God? I listened to something that challenged that idea. I’d be curious what you think.”
A difficult question

Neighbor: If God is good, why does He allow children to suffer?

You: That’s a really good question. I’m not sure how I’d answer that right now. Let me think about it and get back to you.

Later: You research the topic and return with a thoughtful resource or response when the pressure is gone.
Stage Two

Ask questions that make the claim do the work.

There is no absolute truth

Friend: There is no such thing as absolute truth.

You: What do you mean by that?

Friend: Everybody has their own truth.

You: Is the statement “there is no absolute truth” absolutely true?

You have not delivered a lecture. The questions themselves expose a tension in the claim.

Morality is subjective

Coworker: Morality is completely subjective.

You: What do you mean by subjective?

Coworker: Everyone decides what’s right and wrong for themselves.

You: Do you think racism or child abuse are just personal opinions?

The question tests whether the person can consistently live with the claim they made.

Religion causes harm

Friend: Religion causes more harm than good.

You: What makes you think that?

Friend: Look at all the wars religion has caused.

You: Which wars are you thinking of specifically?

You are asking the person to support and define the claim rather than accepting a slogan at face value.

Stage Three

Combine questions with clear explanation.

Science disproves God

Coworker: Science disproves God.

You: What do you mean by “disproves”?

Coworker: We don’t need God to explain things anymore.

You: Science is excellent at explaining natural processes, but do you think it can answer every type of question—like whether human life has objective value?

Now questions and explanation work together to distinguish scientific questions from philosophical ones.

All religions teach the same thing

Friend: All religions basically teach the same thing.

You: What do you mean by that?

Friend: They all teach people to be good.

You: Many encourage moral behavior, but they make very different claims about God, reality, and salvation. Those claims cannot all be true in the same way.

You first clarify the claim, then provide a concise correction.

You don’t need God to be moral

Friend: You don’t need God to be moral.

You: I agree. The question isn’t whether atheists can behave morally. The question is whether objective moral obligations can exist if God does not.

Knowledge lets you identify an important distinction without becoming combative.

Remember the long game

A good conversation does not have to finish anything.

A question can be enough. A follow-up can be enough. A resource can be enough. The goal is not to force every conversation toward a conclusion—it is to faithfully plant, water, and continue engaging over time.

If you reach the edge of your knowledge or comfort at any stage, you can always say, “Let me think about that and get back to you.” That is not failure. It is part of the model.

Take the next step

You do not need the perfect words.

Ask one meaningful question. Listen carefully. Notice where the conversation goes. Then use the rest of the toolkit to prepare, follow up, and continue the relationship.
